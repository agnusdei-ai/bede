## B01 — Observation, inference and organization

A measurement records an observation under specified conditions: “this blade is 6.2cm long using this ruler.” “The plant is healthy” is an interpretation requiring more evidence. Record method, units and repeats so another person can understand the measurement. A controlled comparison changes a proposed cause while keeping relevant conditions similar; a convenience sample is not automatically representative.

Cells are units of living organization. In multicellular organisms, specialized cells can form tissues, organs and interacting organ systems. An organism is not just a list of its parts. A leaf-length category is a measurement rule, not a verified species name. Support: S1 Cell; S2 Structure; sampling guidance is original course methodology.

**Check/key:** Is “long leaves prove good health” warranted? No: length alone does not establish health or a cause. Name a method/control and another observation needed. Link: investigation 1; document 04 week 1; document 05 science diagnostic.

## B02 — Water, pH and the limits of an indicator model

Water is a neutral molecule with an uneven distribution of electrical charge. In an O–H bond the shared electrons are drawn more toward oxygen. Represent oxygen's end as partially negative (δ−) and each hydrogen's end as partially positive (δ+), **not** as separate full-charge ions. The molecule's bent arrangement gives it a negative side and a positive side. Opposite partial charges on neighboring molecules can attract; an attraction involving the hydrogen of one water molecule and the oxygen region of another is a hydrogen bond. This is different from the covalent O–H bonds holding one molecule together. These interactions help explain water's behavior as a cellular solvent.

**Original paper model, no experiment:** draw two bent H–O–H molecules, put δ− beside each O and δ+ beside each H, then turn the second so an H faces the first molecule's O. Mark their intermolecular attraction with a dotted line and each intramolecular covalent bond with a solid line. Labels represent a simplified charge-distribution model, not two substances or a claim that water is an ion.

In aqueous solutions lower pH indicates more acidic conditions. For this introductory concentration model, pH is the negative base-ten logarithm of hydrogen-ion concentration: pH6 corresponds to ten times the hydrogen-ion concentration of pH7, not “one more unit of acid.” No logarithm calculations are required, and no exact sample pH can be recovered from the course's invented indicator colors.

An indicator's appearance must be related to known comparison conditions before it supports a pH inference. Invented color descriptions without calibration do not establish exact pH or even reliable acid/base identity. The course investigation is paper-only; this note authorizes no chemical handling. Support: S14 for polarity/attraction; S13 for the concentration/logarithmic relationship; S3 for general pH terminology. The cellular glossary pages are not claimed to establish these chemical mechanisms.

**Check/key:** Is an entire water molecule an ion because it has partial charges? No: partial charge distribution does not change its net neutrality. Does the dotted intermolecular line represent the same bond as an O–H line within a molecule? No: distinguish hydrogen bonding from the covalent O–H bond. Which has greater hydrogen-ion concentration, pH6 or7? pH6, by tenfold in this model. “Blue proves pH9” still fails without calibration and controlled viewing. Record invented observations as supplied example data. Link: investigation2 and document04 week2; these checks replace part of day1's instruction, not extra homework.

## B03 — Cells and what a microscope resolves

A membrane separates the cell interior from its environment while allowing regulated exchange. Cytoplasm contains cellular components and many reactions. In eukaryotic cells the nucleus contains most DNA; mitochondria participate in energy transfer. Plant cells additionally have a cell wall, and many plant cells that perform photosynthesis contain chloroplasts. Not every plant tissue has visible chloroplasts.

A reference sketch is a model, not a record of what a learner saw. Draw only resolved structures in the observational record; label an unresolved nucleus “not resolved,” not “absent.” Onion bulb epidermis is not a reason to invent chloroplast observations. Support: S1/S2; S4 context for photosynthesis.

**Check/key:** A clear cell boundary does not prove a nucleus was visible. Keep an original model sketch separate from the actual microscope drawing. Link: investigation 3; Q1 drawing assessment.

## B04 — Diffusion and osmosis

Random molecular motion can produce net diffusion from higher to lower concentration when conditions allow. Osmosis is net water movement across a selectively permeable barrier in response to differences in water availability; dissolved solutes affect that availability. The statement “all particles move only one way” confuses net movement with individual motion.

Potato tissue contains many cells and other materials, not one perfect membrane. A mass increase is consistent with net water uptake, but cutting, blotting and timing can affect measured change. Percent mass change is 100(final−initial)/initial, with nonzero initial mass. Support: S2 movement/osmosis; S1 membrane.

**Check/key:** 5.0g→5.3g is +6%, not proof of a particular molecular pathway. Give one measurement control. Link: investigation 4 and document 05 week 4 report.

## B05 — Metabolism, energy and enzymes

Metabolism includes linked chemical reactions. Some reactions release energy that can support other processes; others require energy input. Cells transfer energy rather than manufacture it. Enzymes are catalysts: they help reactions proceed faster by lowering activation barriers, not by changing the overall energy difference between starting and ending states. Most enzymes are proteins; function depends on molecular structure and conditions such as temperature and pH.

**Original paper energy profile:** use hypothetical energy units, not measurements. Put reactants at10 and products at4. Draw one path rising to18 before falling to4, and a catalyzed path rising only to14 before falling to the **same**4. The starting-to-peak barrier is8 versus4 units; the reactant-to-product difference remains−6 on both paths. The enzyme does not supply those6 units or move the endpoints. This model represents a rate-related barrier, not actual time, temperature or a complete molecular mechanism.

An energy diagram needs labeled inputs, transfers and outputs; an arrow alone does not show an observed mechanism. Support: S1 Enzyme for basic catalysis; S15 for activation barriers and unchanged thermodynamic endpoints. S1/S2 alone are not claimed to support the energy-profile mechanism. No matches, heating or chemical demonstration is part of this paper model.

**Check/key:** For the paper profile, barriers are8 and4; both paths have change4−10=−6. “An enzyme makes energy” is wrong: catalysis changes the pathway/rate, not conservation of energy or the endpoint difference. Link: document04 week5 energy model/design; use this within the existing instruction block.

## B06 — Respiration, fermentation and a proxy

Cellular respiration transfers chemical energy from organic molecules into usable cellular forms, including ATP, with heat released. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen; an overall glucose model produces carbon dioxide and water. Breathing exchanges gases at the organism level and is not identical to cellular respiration.

Yeast can also obtain usable energy through alcoholic fermentation, producing carbon dioxide and ethanol. A foam-height comparison is an indirect proxy affected by gas production, mixing, cup shape and bubble stability. It cannot determine which pathway supplied every bubble or measure total respiration. Support: S1 Mitochondria; S2 Function; S3 MeSH fermentation; S12 independent yeast research.

**Check/key:** Control yeast amount, water, temperature, vessel and time; do not conclude a measured CO₂ volume from foam height alone. Link: investigation 6; document 05 week 6 data check. Open cups only; no ingestion or closed fermentation.

## B07 — Photosynthesis and plant mass

Photosynthesis transfers light energy into chemical energy stored in organic molecules. Its overview model uses carbon dioxide and water and releases oxygen. Carbon in new plant organic matter is largely derived from carbon dioxide; soil minerals remain necessary, but soil is not the main source of plant dry mass. Fresh mass also includes water.

Plants respire as well as photosynthesize. The two processes are connected but are not identical reverse pathways. A supplied-data model illustrates a relationship, not proof that the learner measured carbon fixation. Support: S4 Fast Carbon Cycle; S10 coral/algal example; S8 plant mineral needs.

**Check/key:** Explain why “plants eat soil and only respire at night” fails. Carbon comes largely from CO₂; respiration occurs day and night. Link: document 04 week 7 model/data; document 05 week 9 inputs/outputs.

## B08 — Cell cycle, mitosis and counting

During interphase a cell grows and, before division, replicates DNA. Mitosis separates replicated chromosomes into new nuclei; cytokinesis divides the cell contents. Regulation matters: cancer involves abnormal regulation, not the mere occurrence of cell division. This is not a diagnostic test.

The fraction of cells visibly dividing in selected fields depends on preparation, visibility, classification and sampling. Uncertain cells must not be quietly forced into a desired category. Support: S5 Mitosis/Chromosome; S2 Function.

**Check/key:** 6/30 =20% of that recorded sample, not 20% of every plant's cells or necessarily 20% of cycle duration. Link: investigation 8; document 04 week 8; document 05 quarter check.

## B10 — Meiosis and chromosome models

Meiosis produces cells with reduced chromosome sets through two divisions after one DNA replication. A homologous pair consists of corresponding chromosomes inherited from the two parents; sister chromatids are the replicated copies of one chromosome. Homologous chromosomes separate in the first division; sister chromatids separate in the second. Crossovers can exchange segments between homologs before segregation, generating new allele combinations. Mitosis generally preserves chromosome number in daughter nuclei; meiosis is not simply two identical mitoses. Support: S5 for broad terms and S16 for the actual replication/segregation/crossover mechanism.

**Original chromosome-card explanation:** start with two homologous pairs. After replication each chromosome has two chromatids, but there are still two pairs. In divisionI each new cell receives one duplicated homolog from each pair. In divisionII its sister chromatids separate, giving products with one chromosome from each original pair. If using colored marks to model a crossover, show an exchanged segment before divisionI; do not count a color change as an extra chromosome. These are paper tokens only, not live cells.

**Check/key:** For a model beginning with two homologous pairs, gametes receive one chromosome from each pair, not both homologs. Tokens are representations, not molecules. Link: investigation 10.

## B11 — A simple inheritance model

Alleles are alternative versions at a genetic location. In a simple diploid, complete-dominance model, Aa and AA can share a phenotype while having different genotypes. Dominant does not mean better, more common or stronger. Two Aa parents give model probabilities 1/4 AA, 1/2 Aa, 1/4 aa per offspring under the stated assumptions, not a guaranteed four-child pattern. Support: S5 Allele/Dominant/Recessive/Genotype/Phenotype.

**Check/key:** Explain why the next offspring's genotype is not forced by the previous three outcomes. Probability predicts distributions, not a quota.

## B12 — Variation beyond simple dominance

Many traits involve multiple genes, environmental influences or interactions. A single dominant/recessive model cannot predict every human characteristic. Mutations can introduce genetic variation; their effects depend on context and may be harmful, neutral or beneficial. Model beans represent alleles only under explicit assumptions. Do not collect classmates' genetic or medical data. Support: S5 Mutation/Phenotype; S6 Gene.

**Check/key:** A neat bean ratio is evidence of the simulation's rules, not an observed human inheritance law. Link: investigation 12.

## B13 — DNA, genes and traits

DNA stores sequence information. A gene is a functional DNA region whose information can contribute to an RNA or protein product. A chromosome contains many genes and other sequences. A trait is not a physical object sitting at one position on a chromosome, and “one gene always makes one trait” is too simple. Support: S6 Gene; S5 Chromosome.

**Check/key:** Distinguish DNA sequence, gene product and observable trait in a three-part original sketch.

## B14 — Transcription and translation

Transcription uses DNA information to make RNA. During translation, ribosomes use a messenger-RNA sequence to assemble an amino-acid chain. The model DNA→RNA→protein is a useful introductory information-flow model, not a claim that every gene produces protein. Changes to a message can have different effects depending on where and how it changes. Support: S6 Transcription/Translation/Gene Expression.

**Check/key:** Paper letters are not nucleotides and a classroom reader is not a ribosome; identify what each token represents and one missing mechanism. Link: investigation 14.

## B15 — Regulation and specialization

Different cell types can use different portions of largely shared genetic information. Regulation affects when and how much a gene is expressed; cells do not need every product at the same level all the time. A measured difference in a product is evidence to interpret, not proof of a single cause without controls. Support: S6 Gene Regulation/Gene Expression.

**Check/key:** Explain how two specialized cell types can differ without claiming every cell has an entirely different genome.

## B16 — Biotechnology claims

Genetic engineering changes genetic material using laboratory methods. A proposed application has separable questions: what was changed, what outcome was measured, how reliable is the evidence, and what ethical or environmental concerns remain? Success in one laboratory condition does not establish universal safety or benefit. This course uses paper comparisons, not genetic modification experiments. Support: S6 Genetic Engineering.

**Check/key:** Separate an empirical outcome claim from a value judgment in the supplied case. Link: investigation 16; Q2 evidence panel.

## B17 — Evolution by natural selection

Evolution is change in inherited population characteristics across generations. When individuals vary in heritable features and those features affect reproductive success in an environment, their frequencies can change through natural selection. Individuals do not acquire a needed inherited adaptation merely by trying. Mutation, migration and drift also matter; not every change is selection. Support: S7 Evolution/Genetic Drift; S5 Mutation; S8 adaptations.

**Check/key:** In a hypothetical seed-color case, distinguish existing variation, selective pressure and population change. Do not claim that a single seed evolved.

An outcome under a model's rules does not independently verify the history of a real population. Support: S7.

## B19 — Relationships and classification

Classification groups organisms using stated evidence. In a rooted relationship diagram, an internal branch point represents a proposed common ancestor, not a modern organism turning into another modern organism. Tip labels identify sampled organisms; the root is the deepest ancestor represented. Similar visible traits alone do not settle branch placement: genetic evidence can revise an appearance-based grouping, as the fern example illustrates. Support: S17's explicit ancestor-node/root account and S18's genetic revision example, with S7 providing broad evolutionary context.

**Original diagram explanation:** write the hypothetical relationship `((A,B),C)` for three contemporary sampled groups. Label the join of A and B as nodeX, and the older join of X with C as rootR. The diagram proposes A/B share a more recent common ancestor with each other than either does with C. It does not mean A became B, C is primitive, or the branch spacing measures time. No external tree image is required.

**Check/key:** Two similar leaves alone do not prove close ancestry or a species name. Explain what additional evidence would help.

## B20 — Plant diversity and field evidence

Plants differ in reproductive and transport structures. This comparison concerns **ferns and flowering seed plants**: ferns have roots, stems and leaves and are vascular plants, but do not produce flowers or seeds; they commonly reproduce by spores and some also reproduce vegetatively. A flowering seed plant reproduces through a different set of structures. Make an original two-column list of these contrasts; do not turn it into a complete classification of all plants. A visible blade shape alone cannot establish which reproductive structures an unobserved plant has. Support: S18's actual fern/flowering-plant comparison, not S8's tree-anatomy page as evidence for an entire plant-group sequence.

Use actual observed traits for the limited field key, preserve “unknown,” and do not collect or handle protected specimens. The paper comparison supplies context, not permission for additional specimen collection or a species-identification claim.

**Check/key:** Record one observed trait and one identity you cannot establish. Link: investigation 20 leaf survey, not a species-certification exercise.

## B21 — Plant transport

In vascular plants, xylem conducts water and dissolved minerals; phloem transports sugars and other organic solutes between sources and sinks. In a tree, sapwood provides water-conducting tissue and living inner bark contains phloem. Evaporation from leaves contributes to water movement; the plant is not a pump with a human heart. The direction and substance carried matter more than drawing all arrows upward. Support: S8 Anatomy of a Tree, B–D and roots/leaves explanation.

**Check/key:** Draw separate labeled water/mineral and sugar paths. Explain why “all transport carries the same substance” fails.

## B22 — Nutrients and study design

Plants need mineral nutrients as well as water, carbon dioxide and light. Both deficiency and excess can interfere with growth; more of a substance is not automatically better. Germination and later growth are distinct outcomes. An experiment needs a defined treatment, outcome, comparable groups and replicates. An example germination table is supplied-data analysis, not growing plants. Support: S8 Soils and Plant Adaptations; S4.

**Check/key:** Name the denominator for germination proportion and distinguish “not germinated by this time” from “dead.” Link: investigation 22; no growing/mold handling.

## B23 — Animal organization and exchange

Multicellular animals have specialized cells and, in many lineages, organized tissues and organs. Exchange surfaces connect an organism to its environment; size and form affect exchange demands. A human organ model is one example, not a ladder ranking all organisms toward humans. Surface area and volume scale differently as size changes. Support: S2 Structure; S9 lungs.

**Check/key:** For a cube, doubling side length multiplies area by4 and volume by8. Explain the implication without claiming every animal is a cube.

## B24 — Ecology and a bounded observation

An organism interacts with living and nonliving parts of its environment. A population concerns one species in a defined place/time; a community includes interacting populations; an ecosystem includes nonliving surroundings as well. Light, moisture, substrate and interactions may covary. A microhabitat observation can describe an association, not automatically establish causation. Support: S10 ecosystem-management explanation; S11.

**Check/key:** Record place at a privacy-safe level, time, conditions and sampling route; identify one uncontrolled factor. Link: investigation 24.

## B25 — Homeostasis and kidneys

Homeostasis regulates internal conditions within workable ranges, not one unchanging value. Negative feedback reduces a deviation through a response. Kidneys filter blood, remove wastes and regulate water/electrolyte balance; they are not simple sieves that discard every small molecule. Many useful substances are returned to the body. ## B26 — Gas exchange and surface area

In human lungs, branching airways end in small air sacs called alveoli.
