# First nine weeks — teaching guide

## Adult preparation and daily rhythm

Read the assigned selections before teaching; obtain the lawful editions in document 02 and review the lab instructions in document 06. Print the original exercises below, a blank world map, graph paper and notebook pages. Keep the answer guidance separate until the learner has attempted the task. No Bede account or AI access is required.

For each 60-minute core session use **10m retrieve/teach, 35m reading or practice, 10m discussion/check, 5m record/plan**, unless a specific timed agenda below replaces it. Every minute includes independent work; teachers need not lecture for an hour. Assign no additional homework beyond these blocks. Day 1–5 mean Monday–Friday in the family model. The co-op can regroup these same minutes into its meeting/home schedule.

Biology day lengths each week are **60, 30, 90, 60, 60m** = 300m. Studio is **30m/day** = 150m, with 5m recall, 20m task and 5m check. PE/health and arts follow the map's daily strands.

If a learner cannot finish the reading in its allocated time, stop at a natural scene boundary. Adult narrates omitted connective plot and marks which excerpts were actually read. Preserve a substantial passage for analysis; reduce the writing length rather than silently adding hours. Preview distressing content; provide the equivalent alternatives in the resources guide.

**Literature weeks 1–4: one reading budget.** Each week assigns exactly four 30m reading/annotation blocks on days 1–4 = **120m within 300m**. Days 1–4 normally use 10m retrieve/teach, 30m reading, 15m discussion/writing, 5m record; day 5 uses 10m retrieval, 35m writing/revision, 10m oral check, 5m record. Week 4 day 4's drafting agenda below replaces that day's normal split. Supported audio, annotation and any rereading share the 120m, not extra slots. Preview these minimum passages first; if whole books cannot fit, use days 1–2 for the first listed book's passage and days 3–4 for the second, narrating connective plot during retrieve/discussion time. Do not spend all the minutes on a book's opening and leave its analytical passage unread.

## Week 1 — What counts as evidence?

### Literature & Composition I — 300m

**Aim:** distinguish what a text says from what a reader infers. Read *Odyssey* books I–II or the logged minimum passages above, in four 30m reading/annotation segments. No fifth reading slot. Materials L.

- **Day 1:** 10m introduce epic/narrator and locate Ithaca; 30m begin I or its minimum passage; 15m ask “What do we actually know about Telemachus, and who says it?”; 5m record one quotation and book/scene. Model an annotation: “speaker → words → inference,” not “this proves he is good.”
- **Day 2:** 10m distinguish plot summary from interpretation; 30m finish I or closely reread its minimum passage; 15m compare the treatment of visitor and suitors using read evidence; 5m write a one-sentence tentative claim. Ask: “Which action supports it? What other reading is possible?”
- **Day 3:** 10m teach claim/evidence/explanation; 30m begin II or its minimum passage; 15m discuss assembly voices; 5m name a voice not heard. Teacher does not equate a character's statement with the author's endorsement.
- **Day 4:** 10m model integrating a short quotation; 30m finish II or closely reread its minimum passage; 15m seminar: “Does speaking publicly change Telemachus's authority?” Require each learner to refer to a read scene and respond to another interpretation; 5m revise the claim.
- **Day 5:** 10m retrieve two scenes; 35m write 200 words, “How does hospitality reveal a power relationship in books I–II?”; 10m oral defense; 5m choose one revision target.

**Formative check:** learner labels one statement observation/textual fact, one inference, one unsupported speculation. Accept differing interpretations with accurate evidence. A strong paragraph connects a specific act of welcome or consumption to power and explains the connection. “Hospitality is important” alone is too general. No model thesis is supplied for copying; adult asks where the evidence would change the claim.

### World History I — 300m

**Read H01:** UNG *World History: Cultures, States, and Societies to 1500* §5.7, first four original author-prose paragraphs only; exclude quoted extracts and footnotes, at most 20m. Reread this selection on days 2–3; the source/causation examples below are original teaching, not additional textbook headings. Materials H; see the replacement register for edition/license.

- **Day 1:** introduce BCE/CE and century notation; read H01; mark Mesopotamia, Nile, Indus, Yellow River, Mesoamerica and Andes on a learner-drawn schematic map using adult geographic guidance. Locate, do not rank. Exit: “A map's projection changes ___.” No textbook map is assigned.
- **Day 2:** reread H01 on literary evidence and limits. Classify an ancient inscription, a modern textbook, an excavated pot and a modern film. Explain that “primary” depends on the question; a modern film can be primary evidence about its own time.
- **Day 3:** revisit H01's source warning; teach that a ruler's claim alone does not establish cause. Original source exercise: “A fictional town record says 40 grain baskets arrived; a ruler claims everyone prospered.” Identify author, purpose, observable claim, unsupported conclusion. Label this **invented practice**, not an ancient document.
- **Day 4:** build chronology: 2500 BCE, 1200 BCE, 500 BCE, 200 CE; add a relative timeline of the fictional town records. Ask why a later copy's date differs from the event described.
- **Day 5:** write 250 words: “What can a source tell us, and what does it leave out?” Attach the map and source log. Oral check: name one additional source needed to assess the prosperity claim.

**Guidance:** chronological order as listed; 500 BCE is sixth century BCE. An inscription is not inherently truthful; the pot can answer material questions but rarely reveals an individual's motives alone. Grain arrivals do not establish distribution, population or wellbeing. Useful additional evidence includes household distribution, wages, population and independent records; no single source proves universal prosperity.

### Latin I — 300m

**Adult input:** D'Ooge §§1–17 only: alphabet, sounds, syllables, quantity and accent; no introductory historical essays. As document 02 explains, claims that Rome ruled the whole ancient world or Latin was the only light of learning are historical textbook advocacy, not facts to teach. Use macrons to mark vowel length, not stress; retain lexical quantities such as *īnsula* and *nārrō* in supplied models. Pick one consistent pronunciation convention and explain that both restored classical and ecclesiastical traditions exist. No penalty for accent variations that preserve taught distinctions.

**Vocabulary:** *puella* girl; *fābula* story; *aqua* water; *terra* land; *nauta* sailor (masculine despite first-declension ending). Recognition-only verbs this week: *amat* “likes/loves,” *portat* “carries.”

- **Day 3:** compare *puella aquam portat* and *aquam puella portat*. Parse *puella* nominative subject and *aquam* accusative object. Word order alone does not determine the roles.
- **Day 4:** practice T1–T3; read aloud; retrieve vocabulary without cards; explain each ending.
- **Day 5:** unaided T4–T5, then oral parsing and correction; reread examples with fluency.

**Original T set:** T1 translate *nauta aquam portat*. T2 translate *puella fābulam amat*. T3 write “The girl carries water.” T4 in *fābulam nauta amat*, who likes what? T5 explain why “aqua” and “aquam” can name the same substance but have different sentence roles.

**Key:** T1 sailor carries water; T2 girl likes the story; T3 *puella aquam portat* (other clear orders acceptable); T4 sailor likes story; T5 the ending signals case/function. No Latin articles correspond exactly to English “a/the”; accept either where context permits.
