# Teaching a child who learns differently

> **Who this is for.** A parent whose child finds part of a lesson harder
> than it should be — reading the screen, getting started, holding onto what
> they just heard, staying with it for an hour, or saying back out loud what
> they clearly understood.
>
> **You do not need a diagnosis to use anything in this document.** If you
> have one, you do not need to share it. If you suspect something and do not
> know, that is a conversation for your pediatrician or an educational
> specialist, and everything here works perfectly well in the meantime.

---

## The honest part first

**Bede is a tutor.** It is not special education, not therapy, and not a
reading intervention. It does not replace a structured literacy program
(Orton-Gillingham and its descendants), speech therapy, or occupational
therapy, and it should not be chosen instead of one. If your child needs that
kind of instruction, they need it, and no setting in this document is a
substitute.

**There is no "dyslexia mode," and there never will be.** Two reasons, and
both matter:

* **Naming a setting after a condition would mean this software was holding a
  diagnosis.** That is yours, and your doctor's. Bede never names, guesses at,
  or implies one — to your child or to you. Everything here is a plain setting
  any parent can turn on for any child, for any reason, including "we tried it
  and it seemed to help."
* **The most-requested feature in a dyslexia mode does not work.**
  Dyslexia-specific fonts — OpenDyslexic and the rest — have been tested and
  do not improve reading rate or accuracy; children sometimes read slightly
  *worse* with them than with ordinary Arial. The International Dyslexia
  Association's own position is that no reliable evidence supports them. Bede
  will not ship you something that does not work just because it is the thing
  everyone asks for.

**What Bede does offer** a child who learns differently is patience that does
not run out, a pace set by them, a subject they can stay on until they have it,
and accommodations held quietly enough that the lesson simply fits.

**What none of it is measured against.** Bede never scores your child. It
reports what it saw in a piece of work, judged against what the task actually
asked for — never against another child, never against what a child that age
"should" do, and never against how your own child did last week. A child asked
for one part of a story who gives one part well has done the work, and is told
so exactly as any other child would be.

## The two panels, and which one does what

Everything below lives on the **Parent Setup** page, inside the student's own
card. Both panels are per-student, so they follow your child to whatever tablet
they sit at.

| Panel | What it changes | Use it when the obstacle is… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What Helps This Child** | What Bede *says* and *asks* | …understanding, starting, holding on, or being asked in a way that lands |
| **Reading and Breaks** | What the screen *looks like*, and how often a pause is offered | …reading the screen itself, or staying with it for an hour |

They are two halves of one idea and they do not overlap. Careful wording
cannot help a child who cannot comfortably read the words; wider letter
spacing cannot help a child who does not know what is being asked.

## Panel 1 — What Helps This Child

Type in, or tap a chip for, anything you already know makes a lesson go better
for this child. Up to ten. It is deliberately capped there: Bede is told to
*follow* these, and a list long enough to dilute each one is worse than a short
list it actually holds.

The chips cover six kinds of obstacle. Your own wording for anything they miss
is kept exactly as you type it.

**Pace and how a lesson comes across**
More time to answer · Shorter passages at a time · Answer out loud instead of
writing · Read the passage aloud to them · Break tasks into one step at a time ·
Frequent short breaks · Repeat instructions before starting · Say numbers and
letters clearly, one at a time

**Knowing what's coming** — for a child who spends part of themselves bracing
for a surprise
Say what's coming next before starting · Keep the same routine each session

**Knowing what's being asked** — Bede's whole method is open Socratic
questioning, which is also the hardest shape to answer when you are not sure
what is wanted
Ask one direct question rather than an open one · Say what a good answer would
include

**Time to think** — different from "more time to answer," which is about how
long your child may take once they have started
Give the question, then quiet time to think

**Moving**
Offer a movement break partway through

**Holding onto it**
Recap what was just covered before asking about it · Let them look back at the
book while telling it back

### Three rules Bede holds while following them

1. **The material and the standard do not change.** This changes *how* a lesson
   is delivered, never *what* is taught and never how the work is judged.
   Removing an obstacle between your child and the material is help; removing
   the material is not.
2. **Your child is never told.** "Let's take this one step at a time" is
   something Bede will say. "Because reading is hard for you" is something it
   will not, ever. Your child experiences a lesson that fits them, not a lesson
   they can tell was adjusted.
3. **No diagnosis, ever.** You have told Bede what helps. You have not told it
   why, that is not its question to answer, and it is not qualified to answer
   it.

## Panel 2 — Reading and Breaks

**Three settings.** Every one starts off, and turning none of them on leaves
the screen exactly as it is today.

**You set them; your child can also adjust the two spacing ones from their
own screen.** The value you choose here is the starting point and applies on
every device. From the reading button in the corner of a lesson, your child
can move the spacing on the tablet in front of them — which matters, because
the child who needs wider spacing usually discovers it mid-page, and asking
them to stop, find you, and wait while you open Parent Setup is asking them
not to bother. Their change stays on that device and never edits your setting,
and if you change the setting yourself, yours takes over again everywhere.
The break checkbox stays yours alone.

**They are not equally well supported, and the panel says so on each one.**
Identical-looking controls would imply equal promises, and "letter spacing has
real evidence, line spacing is general guidance" is precisely the thing a
parent has no way to know. The evidence, including what argues against it, is written up in
[`ACCESSIBILITY_RESEARCH.md`](ACCESSIBILITY_RESEARCH.md).

### Space between letters — Normal / Wide / Wider

**The one with real evidence behind it, and the one to try first.** In dyslexic
children aged 8-14, extra letter spacing **doubled reading accuracy** and made
reading **more than 20% faster**, and the effect held across children reading
two different languages. The mechanism is *crowding*: a letter is harder to
identify when it is close to its neighbours, and some readers are affected by
that far more than others.

It widens the space between **words** at the same time, deliberately. Pushing
letters apart without pushing words apart makes it harder to see where one word
ends and the next begins — your child would gain clarity inside a word and lose
the boundary between two, which for a child still assembling words is a poor
trade.

*One honest note.* A published objection to that study argues the benefit may
belong to struggling readers **generally** rather than to dyslexia
specifically. If that is right, it is an argument for exactly this shape — a
plain setting any parent can turn on, rather than one locked behind a diagnosis
a family has to obtain first.

### Space between lines — Normal / Relaxed / Loose

Helps some readers keep their place when their eye travels back to the start of
the next line. **Honestly, this one rests on general readability guidance
rather than on a study of children like yours** — one study that looked
specifically at line spacing for fifth graders found no effect. Worth trying.
Not worth promising.

### Text size — the **T** button, not this panel

Text size is not one of these settings, and that is deliberate. You already
have it: the small **T** button in the top-right corner of every screen,
which scales the whole app from 87.5% up to 175%. It is one tap from wherever
your child is, and **your child can reach it themselves** — which for a
preference is better than a setting only you can change.

A per-student copy of it briefly existed in this panel and was removed rather
than left as a second, weaker control for the same thing.

The evidence supports that placement too. Bigger is not reliably better, and
the direction reverses with age: in one study smaller type **raised**
comprehension for fifth graders while **lowering** it for second graders. So
it is a preference, and a preference belongs where the reader can set it.

### Offer a break every 20 minutes — a checkbox

Bede already offers younger children an optional pause at the 20- and
40-minute marks of each hour. It is a dismissible banner, never a forced stop:
"Yes, let's pause" starts a break your child ends themselves; "No thanks, keep
going" waves it off until the next mark.

That offer used to be available **only to grades K-3**, which meant a
twelve-year-old who does better stopping every twenty minutes could not have it
at all — not because anyone weighed it and decided against, but because the only
way in was being under nine. This checkbox makes it available at any grade.

**It is not a claim that more breaks improve attention.** The research on
physical activity and attention in children with ADHD is genuinely encouraging,
but it points at structured, cognitively engaging activity a couple of times a
week — and its frequency finding actually runs the *other* way from the
intuition. Offering a pause inside a lesson is a different thing, and Bede does
not dress it up as an intervention. What this checkbox does is remove an age
limit on a choice that is yours to make.

**It cannot touch the mandatory break.** The ten-minute break every hour stays
exactly as it is, for everyone: this setting cannot shorten it, skip it, delay
it, or extend a suggestion past it.

## Telling it back: narration has more than one route

Narration — your child saying back, in their own words, what they just learned —
is the heart of this method. It is also the hardest single thing in it: free
recall, out loud, with nothing in front of you, which is the most demanding
shape a task takes here.

**So it is the place where a child who cannot yet produce it is most likely to
be quietly given less to learn instead of another way in.** Bede does not do
that. If free recall is out of reach on a given day, it takes another route,
and there are five:

1. **Tell it back with the book open.**
2. **Tell one part** — not the whole thing.
3. **Answer two or three concrete questions** instead of retelling freely.
4. **Draw it first**, then talk about the drawing.
5. **Bede starts the retelling and hands it over mid-sentence.**

Every one of them still ends with your child telling it back. Every one is a
change to *how* they tell it, never to *what* they were expected to learn.

**And what comes back is judged against what was actually asked for.** A child
asked for one part who gives one part well has met the standard, and is
celebrated exactly as any other child would be. A route is never offered as a
consolation prize, and your child is never told the narration was shaped for
them.

You do not have to configure this. Setting "Let them look back at the book while
telling it back," or anything else in Panel 1, points Bede at these routes.

## "My child…" — what to try first

| If… | Start with |
| --- | --- |
| …reads slowly, or sounds out words they know | **Space between letters: Wide.** The best-supported setting here. |
| …loses their place, or re-reads the same line | **Space between lines: Relaxed**, and letters Wide. |
| …squints, or holds the tablet close | The **T** button, top-right of any screen. Also worth an eye exam. |
| …can't get started on an open question | "Ask one direct question rather than an open one" and "Say what a good answer would include". |
| …answers before thinking, or freezes | "Give the question, then quiet time to think". |
| …understands but can't retell it | "Let them look back at the book while telling it back" and "Recap what was just covered before asking about it" — then let Bede pick a narration route. |
| …is anxious at the start of a session | "Say what's coming next before starting" and "Keep the same routine each session". |
| …fades after twenty minutes | **Offer a break every 20 minutes**, plus "Offer a movement break partway through". |
| …writes slowly or painfully | "Answer out loud instead of writing". |
| …misses spoken instructions | "Repeat instructions before starting" and "Say numbers and letters clearly, one at a time". |

**Change one thing at a time and give it a week.** You are the one who can tell
whether it helped; nothing in Bede measures that for you, on purpose.

## What Bede will never do

* **Never name, guess at, or imply a diagnosis** — to your child or to you.
* **Never tell your child a lesson was adjusted for them**, or give them a
  reason for it.
* **Never lower the standard.** More time is more time; it is not an easier
  question or a softer judgement of the answer.
* **Never hurry your child, time them, or mention pace to them.** When Bede
  notes that work is coming easily now, that is a note to you about the work,
  not a stopwatch shown to your child.
* **Never score your child as a person.** It reports on a piece of work, and
  nowhere in Bede is there a number about who your child is.

---

## What this cannot tell you

* **No setting here has been tested with a real child using Bede.** Every
  finding behind them comes from reading research on other materials. Whether
  letter spacing helps your child read *this* screen is unmeasured, which is
  why "try it for a week" is the honest instruction.
* **The specific values are judgements.** "Wide" and "Wider" bracket a range
  similar to the study's, in proportions that suit a screen. They are not a
  reproduction of its stimulus.
* **This is not an assessment.** Nothing in these panels tells you whether your
  child has a learning difference. If you are wondering, ask someone qualified
  to answer.
